Why Us

Translation alone won’t get your product used in a new market.

When the market and users change, the same product is used in a different context. How people work, how they make sense of a product, and how they judge trust all shift with it—and teams that built for the Korean market often find these differences hard to see from the inside.

Translation-first localization

The UI copy is now in English, but onboarding still assumes a Korean user, so first-time users don’t grasp the product’s value.

Can a first-time user understand the value and get started?

Different ways of working

Core workflows don’t fit how local teams divide roles and handle approvals, so the product isn’t used in day-to-day work.

Do core workflows match how target users actually work?

Structure and wording

Menu structure and button labels don’t make the next step clear.

Do the screen structure and wording clearly guide the next action?

No clear priorities

There seems to be a lot to fix, but the team can’t decide what to tackle first before launch.

Which UX problems should be fixed first before launch?

The examples above illustrate common types of trial and error; they are not specific client cases.

  1. 01

    Home Healthcare SaaS · 2025–2026Scheduling UX for a U.S. home healthcare SaaS

    Designed complex eligibility conditions, scheduling rules, and exceptions so users could understand them and act right away.

    Key design decision Instead of exposing internal rules and codes, a calendar of bookable dates and clear status indicators let users decide their next step.

    • 5.8 → to 8.2

      User rating Confidence in planning schedules, measured in the pilot

    • 180 sec → to 60 sec

      Work outcome Task time after several improvements were applied together

    • 35% less

      Work outcome Manual validation in the AI-assisted scheduling workflow

    • 3,000+

      Adoption scope Clinicians using the AI scheduling recommendation experience

    Read the full case
    Project context and problem
    An AI scheduling platform that U.S. home healthcare operations teams use to coordinate clinician visits. Schedulers had to weigh patient eligibility, visit frequency, and staff availability together, which led to repeated questions and confusion.
    Rachel’s role and responsibilities
    • Joined as the founding designer (Senior Product Designer).
    • Defined the product design vision and strategy for the core AI scheduling platform with the CEO, translating business goals into end-to-end design direction from 0 to 1.
    • Built the company’s first Figma design system with the CTO and 6 front-end engineers.
    • Established the company’s first end-to-end design process shared by design, product, and engineering.
    Key design decisions and why
    • Because overlapping eligibility, visit-frequency, and availability rules caused repeated questions and confusion, the design shows bookable dates and status first instead of explaining the rules.
    • Replaced internal codes with wording users could understand.
    • To improve both decision speed and confidence, defined how AI-generated schedule recommendations are surfaced, validated, and adjusted.
    Screens and workflows changed
    • A calendar of bookable dates and schedule status indicators
    • UI wording that replaces internal codes
    • A flow for reviewing, validating, and adjusting AI schedule recommendations
    Results
    • User rating Schedule-planning confidence 5.8 → 8.2 in the pilot
    • Work outcome Task time 180 sec → 60 sec after several improvements were applied together
    • Work outcome Manual validation reduced by 35% in the AI-assisted scheduling workflow
    • Adoption scope Scheduling recommendation experience used by 3,000+ clinicians
    • Adoption scope Design system adopted by all 23 developers as the org-wide standard
    • Work outcome Release cycles accelerated by 50%+ after the design system was adopted
  2. 02

    Global Pharma · Enterprise Monitoring · 2024Operations monitoring and incident management UX for a global pharmaceutical company

    Connected severity, scope of impact, and investigation priority across a large volume of information.

    Key design decision Restructured information scattered across 8 dashboards around severity and downstream impact, so teams could first decide what to investigate.

    • 30 min → to 3 min

      Work outcome Incident investigation time reported in the project

    • 80% faster

      Work outcome Incident triage time after redesigning decision-support workflows and signal display

    • 40% faster

      Work outcome Ticket processing time in developers’ internal tools

    Read the full case
    Project context and problem
    An enterprise monitoring platform supporting vaccine operations, used by the company’s engineering teams. To investigate an incident, operators had to move between 8 dashboards to work out severity and impact.
    Rachel’s role and responsibilities
    • Joined as a contract Senior Product Designer through a UX design agency.
    • Designed a generative-AI incident management system with a global IT company’s AI/ML teams, translating model outputs into actionable signals for enterprise users.
    • Conducted 15 stakeholder interviews to define how AI insights such as risk predictions, alerts, and confidence levels are surfaced and prioritized.
    Key design decisions and why
    • Because operators had to piece together severity and impact across 8 dashboards themselves, the information architecture was redesigned around severity and downstream impact.
    • Rather than listing risk predictions, alerts, and confidence levels with equal weight, set criteria for showing them by investigation priority.
    • Aligned with engineering on a list-browsing approach that accounts for performance.
    Screens and workflows changed
    • An incident investigation flow centered on severity and downstream impact
    • Decision-support screens that surface AI insights by priority
    • Performance-aware incident list browsing
    Results
    • Work outcome Incident investigation time 30 min → 3 min, as reported in the project
    • Work outcome Incident triage time reduced by 80% after redesigning decision-support workflows and clarifying signals
    • Work outcome Developer internal-tool ticket processing time reduced by 40%
    • Research scope 15 stakeholder interviews
  3. 03

    Global Tech · Korean Localization · 2019Korean product localization for a global tech company

    Adapted the product experience to the language and its context of use.

    Key design decision Went beyond translated copy, adapting name, date, and time formats as well as typography and spacing to the Korean context of use.

    • 6 weeks

      Project length Localization period in 2019, with a production agency and the client’s Korea and U.S. teams

    Read the full case
    Project context and problem
    A project adapting content for a 5K-display desktop product to the Korean market, keeping the global brand’s expression while making Korean copy and layout read naturally.
    Rachel’s role and responsibilities
    Over 6 weeks in 2019, worked with a global production agency in the U.K. and the client’s Korea and U.S. teams to localize UX copy and design assets and support communication between teams.
    Key design decisions and why
    • Because both the global brand’s expression and natural Korean had to be preserved, name, date, and time formats were matched to Korean conventions, not just the copy.
    • For the same reason, typography and spacing were adjusted for Korean text.
    • Supported communication so the U.K., Korea, and U.S. teams stayed aligned.
    Screens and workflows changed
    • Korean UX copy
    • Name, date, and time formats
    • Localized layouts reflecting Korean typography and spacing
    Deliverables
    • UX copy and design assets tailored to the Korean market
    • Localized layouts
